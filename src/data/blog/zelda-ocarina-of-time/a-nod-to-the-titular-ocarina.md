It's definitely not something I consciously noticed when playing this game originally, but then again I was also about 12. But the audio design is spot on: from the simplicity of the ambient to the excellent score, it's a bit of a triumph.

## Top notes

While traversing around, especially in friendlier, greener areas, there's a hopeful little score that starts as the sun comes up that is a proper joy. I love it. This is a mood that is repeated and present in most of the music throughout the game.

Everything, from the "danger is present" noises to the anticipation that certain enemy attack patterns come with is put together fantastically well. Now I'm older, I think I'm able to appreciate and understand the difference these things make far more consciously than when I was younger. Back then, I assume my thinking stopped somewhere around the "game good" mark, never getting much deeper into it.

And this is true of the "gimick" of the game, the Ocarina itself. It's used as a _mostly_ simple enough puzzle tool. The game makes it relatively clear when it's expecting you to play a song, but there are enough occasions where it's a little more oblique that I find myself whipping it out and trying something anyway.

But given it's a game with a musical instrument as a key tool, it was important that the "songs" you play on it work well. And boy do they. There are little things like the Sun Song mirroring the actual music that I noted that plays as the sun comes up: it's the sort of techniques you find in musical theatre and some films, where the reprise foreshadows various events, circumstances or possibilities.

It's brilliantly done and even more impressively, many of the songs are incredibly enjoyable (I'm looking at you, Saria's Song).

It's worth remembering that it was a relatively new innovation that you could have fully formed music to begin with, the generation prior the N64 was mostly 32bit and therefore hardware limited. To bust out a title like this, with such a strong soundscape is truly an impressive thing.
